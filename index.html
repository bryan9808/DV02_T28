Hospitalised road crash injuries in Australia, 2011–2021

Who gets hurt and where

Each year, tens of thousands of people survive a road crash but are admitted to hospital. This story follows them: who they are, what they collided with, and where they live.

How many people are hospitalised each year?

Hospitalisations rose about 16% between 2011 and 2021, peaking at 39,866 in 2019 before a dip in 2020.

Dashed lines mark changes in how Victoria (2012) and New South Wales (2017) counted admissions, so drops at these points are partly due to counting, not fewer injuries.

Who is injured?

Pedal cyclists had the biggest rise: 1,087 more hospitalisations in 2021 than in 2017, while car passengers and pedestrians fell.

What did they collide with?

For motorcyclists and cyclists, the most common crash involved no one else: about 4 in 10 cases. Almost 9 in 10 injured pedestrians were hit by a car, pick-up truck or van.

Where are people hospitalised?

Victoria treats the most people, but once population is taken into account the Northern Territory has the highest rate: about 2.6 times that of New South Wales.

Who is most at risk?

Adjusting for how many years each age group covers, young adults aged 17–25 stand out. And people living in remote areas are hospitalised at about 1.6 times the rate of people in major cities.

What this means

Key points

  • Non-fatal road injuries are a large and slowly growing burden: almost 40,000 hospitalisations a year.
  • Cyclists are the group with a clear rise, and most rider injuries involve no other party.
  • Raw counts hide risk: the Northern Territory and remote areas have the highest rates.

Limits of the data

  • Victoria (2012) and New South Wales (2017) changed how admissions were counted.
  • The data shows patterns, not causes; a dip “coinciding” with COVID-19 is not proof of cause.
  • Some cases have an unknown counterparty, state or residence.
